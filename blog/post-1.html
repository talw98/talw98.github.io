Why I'm learning to build infrastructure, not just models

Most of my research day is spent thinking about models: architectures for segmenting tumors, loss functions that behave on messy clinical data, ways to validate results a radiologist would trust. That work is deep, but it's also narrow — it rarely touches the systems that get a model from a notebook into something other people can actually rely on.

That gap is part of why I joined an EU hackathon this year, working on an open-source model orchestration layer rather than anything related to my thesis. It was a deliberate choice. I already know how to design and evaluate a model; I don't have much practice building the infrastructure around one — the routing, the failure handling, the parts that make a system usable rather than just correct.

The uncomfortable part

The first few days were humbling. Reading through the codebase, I kept reaching for research instincts that didn't apply: there's no held-out test set for "does this API gracefully degrade when a provider times out." The skills that make a good research paper — rigor, careful comparison, precise language — don't automatically transfer to writing resilient orchestration logic. I had to relearn what "good work" looks like in this context.

Practical engineering skill isn't a byproduct of research skill. It has to be built separately, on purpose.

What's transferring

Some things did carry over. The habit of reading someone else's code closely before touching it — the same instinct I use reading a paper's methods section before trusting its results. Comfort with ambiguity, since clinical imaging data is never as clean as a benchmark dataset. And the willingness to be a beginner again, which research forces on you constantly if you're doing it honestly.

I'll be writing more here as the hackathon project develops — partly to track my own learning, partly because "here's what I got wrong this week" is more useful to write down than it is to keep in my head.
